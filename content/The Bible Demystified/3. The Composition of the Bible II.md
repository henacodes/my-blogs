Earlier on the series I pointed out that technically there is no such thing as "the Bible." Other than the fact that many Christians have different sets of canons or list of books they consider are holy scriptures, we also have the Jewish people who wholly reject the entire portion of the Christian bible we would today call the New Testament. For Jewish people, their bible is composed of the books that we find in the Old Testament of the Protestant canon. And in this piece of article we will be discussing the history of the composition of this second section of the Christian Bible.

Unlike their position regarding on the Old Testament, the majority of sects of Christianity today have similar list of books when it comes to the New Testament, with the notable exception of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church canon which has two versions of the New Testament, the broader of which contains 35 books. The 27 books canon of the New Testament that all Christians agree on has four gospels, one narrative book (Acts of the Apostles), 14 letters that are believed to have been written by Paul, other letters by multiple authors and one book of apocalypse.

## The Gospels

The word gospel comes from the greek word "_euangelion_" which literally means Godspel, another term for good announcement. It is a key word in Mark's opening sentence: 'the beginning of the good news of Jesus Christ'. The 'good news' or 'gospel', then, is the story of Jesus of Nazareth in Galilee, who lived from approximately 4 BCE to CE 30.

As mentioned above we have four gospels in the Christian bibles. Three of them— Matthew, Mark and Luke— are usually refers to as the "synoptic" gospels. "Synopsic" is used to mean that the gospels can be seen together side by side since they basically have the same sequence of stories. The other left out gospel is John, which is places last even in the Bible (poor John). The reason John is left out from the rest of the gospel is because it can not be seen together with the other three gospels, and it's narrative sequence is in-congruent with the others.

> In John the 'cleansing of the temple' comes at the beginning (2.13-22), in the synoptics at the end (Mark 11.15-19 and parallels.). In the synoptics there is a last supper at which Jesus uses bread and wine as symbols (Mark 14.22-25 and parr.), in John at the last supper Jesus washes the disciples' feet, but says nothing about the bread and wine (ch. 13); John has, instead, a discourse on Jesus as the bread of life, which is placed early in Jesus' 'career.[^1]

### The Origins of the Synoptic Gospels

I have made a conscious decision to exclude the gospel of John in this piece, for one thing, because I am not myself well-read on Johanine scholarship, and two because scholarship around John is notoriously fragmented and has little to no consensus compared to the synoptic scholarship.

On the critically acclaimed show The Chosen, which is based on a conglomerate story of the canonical gospels, we see the disciple Matthew depicted as writing his gospels in multiple episodes. The idea behind it is that Matthew started writing his gospel back when he was still following Jesus. Seen through dramatic lens, it makes perfect sense to depict Matthew as such, but scholars of the gospels have long reached pretty solid consensus that the the Gospel of Matthew, as opposed to the traditional attribution to Matthew, is written anonymously and that we do not know who actually wrote it. And unfortunately, that is the case for the rest of the canonical gospels too.

Just like it was the case for the previous discussion of authorship of the Pentateuch, I wouldn't be surprised if you raise your eyebrow when hearing that the gospels were written anonymously, after all we literally have "The Gospel cccording to Matthew" , "... to Mark" and "... to Luke" as prefaces of the gospels. The problem is that these superscripts that are coming printed without Bible today come only after their composition, and not along with it. The author of Mark, for example, did not write, 'The Gospel according to Mark' , but simply 'the beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God' (Mark 1.1).

#### But Why?

The question is why would we reject one of the most well-established church traditions? Here is the thing. The first person to refer to the gospels by the name we use today who also **had the gospels in the form as we have them today** was the church father Saint Irenaeus (Note the bold part. It will be an important factor when discussing other sources). Writing around the year 180, Irenaeus names all the gospels in his Against Heresies[^3] and used them as well elsewhere in his work.

Thirty or so years earlier from Iranaeus, we have Justin Martyr writing around 150 CE. He quoted sayings of Jesus from what he called 'the Memoirs of the Apostles'. Unlike Iranaeus, he didn't attribute the work he was quoting from. Complicating this is the fact that Justin did not quote the sayings precisely as we have them in our gospels. His quotations often appear to be a conflation of multiple gospel accounts. The two likely explanations for this are that either he quoted from memory and naturally conflated them or that he had a different sayings document altogether. In any case, what we can note from Justin's use of the gospels is that he did not name the gospels ( either because he was not aware of who wrote them or because it didn't appear to him significant to name them ) and that he was not concerned to keep them distinct, quoting precisely from one or the other. [^4]

When we go back yet another few years earlier, we find Papias, the Bishop of Hierapolis. He is the first person to have ever associated the names of the gospels' writers that we have today with books, namely Mark and Matthew. He said, writing around 125-150 CE, "Mark became Peter's interpreter and wrote accurately all that he remembered, not, indeed, in order, of the things said or done by the Lord." About Matthew he said 'Matthew collected the oracles in the Hebrew language, and each interpreted them as best he could'.[^5]

This is where the question of whether or not the person had the same gospel as the ones we have today becomes important? We should note first that Papias stands alone in a long standing tradition where quotation of Jesus' saying is accompanied by no authorial attribution, starting from the Didache, a first century Christian document to Clement, Igntaius and others. "There is no sign of a continuing tradition assigning the gospels to Matthew, Mark and Luke." Anyway coming back to the question at hand, Papias described Matthew as a sayings gospel written in Hebrew. But that description fails to align well with what we know of the Gospel of Matthew we have today, because Matthew is neither a sayings gospel, nor is it written in Hebrew. Our Matthew is heavily a narrative gospel as much as a sayings one. Although the author shows remarkable knowledge of Judaism, the book doesn't seem to be written in Hebrew. All this shows us that when Papias spoke of a book by Matthew, he probably was referring to another document that is different from the Gospel of Matthew we have today.

How about Papias' reference to Mark? Well we can assert with a reasonable certainty that he was indeed referring to the Gospel of Mark as we have it today. The author of Luke prefaces his gospel by saying that he had followed all things 'accurately' and that he would write an 'orderly account' (Luke 1.3), thus implicitly criticizing his sources. Interestingly Papias seems to be aware of this latent criticism and was trying to defend Mark when he said ", he followed Peter, who adapted his teaching to the needs of his hearers, but with no intention of giving a connected account of the Lord's discourses, so that Mark committed no error." To put the argument straightforward, we know that Mark was one of Luke's sources, which we will discuss shortly. And Luke seems to criticize his sources as being un-orderly. But Papias comes in clutch and justifies Mark's un-orderliness by saying that he wrote not in order because he constrained with the needs of his hearers.

Based on our analysis of the external evidence for the authorial attributions of the synoptics, we can have two general considerations, which are

1. Most scholars consider the range of date in which the synoptic gospels were composed to be between 65 to 85 CE. If this dating is indeed true, it stands to reason that the gospels were being used for about 50 years without any authorial attribution. But in order to consider the decade-late attribution to be based on any knowledge, we would have to suppose that early Christians transmitted authors' names, but no surviving literary work from that time mentions any, which is very awkward to position to be at. "The early Christians seem genuinely not to have cared who wrote the gospels, and it is difficult to combine a theory of carefully maintained tradition with the fact of literary silence. This consideration counts in favour of the view that the names which were attached to the gospels were not based on an unbroken tradition." [^6]
2. When we look at the attribution the early church fathers gave to the canonical gospel, we can see that their attributions —unless were not based on historical constraint— can not be merely explained by simple guess. For example look at the second gospel's (the Gospel of Mark) attribution to Mark. If the fathers were merely dependent on hunch, they would have attributed that gospel to directly Peter,rather than his disciple Mark, and similarly to Paul rather than his companion Luke. They must had more than a mere hunch for them to ignore such pillars of the church and give attribution to their respective disciples.

The the second observation, as Sanders and Davies put it ( in their work Studying the Synoptic Gospels, for anybody who hasn't been checking the footnotes), is at best plausible and isn't entirely persuasive. They say

> One can readily offer an alternative explanation. Irenaeus (and others) did not have a clue where the Gospel of Mark came from or who wrote it. They loved the gospel and found in its rough and plain prose an echo of an earlier day and a place where Greek was not fluent. They wished to assign a gospel to Peter, and the apparently simple gospel now called Mark was the likeliest candidate. They could not, however, attribute it to Peter himself, since it had been around for almost a hundred years without having been ascribed to him. They put together Acts 12.12 (Peter went to Mark's mother's house in Jerusalem) and I Peter 5.13 ('my son Mark') and concluded that the historical person Mark could have written the gospel.

And funny enough, they continue to propose yet another problem against their own proposal. "if the second-century fathers were just guessing, why did they not pick Silas (or Silvanus) as the author of the Second Gospel? He seems actually to have written I Peter (see 5.12), and he was also active in Jerusalem (Acts 15.22)."

The problem with any proposal that is based on an idea of what an ancient person could have done or could have thought is that they are entire based on, at best, informed guess. They can not raise above the level of plausibility. After discussing those hunches regarding why the fathers picked Matthew, Luke and John as authors, Sanders and Davies conclude that "fact of anonymous authorship and circulation is the most telling and that their judgment is that "the gospels were truly anonymous and that the authors were not known outside their own immediate circle." [^7]

Similar explanations as to why the fathers picked any of the people a hypothesis which attempts to make sense of the evidence, but which in the nature of the case can be no more than a hypothesis.

### The Synoptic Problem

The three canonical gospels or the synoptic gospels are called as such because, as we mentioned before, they can be read side by side. But the more surprising thing about them is they are remarkably similar in their content and how they present their story. Their similarity is so blatant that scholars have reached to a unanimous consensus they there is certainly a literary interdependence between them, by that we mean that one copied another.

When we study the synoptic gospels entirely, we will find out a couple of basic facts about their relationship. Here are some of them

- The same passages often appear in all three synoptics. These passages constitute the triple tradition.
- In the triple tradition, they often agree on the relative order of the narrative. But when they disagree, Matthew and Mark never agree against Mark. When Matthew disagrees with Mark, Luke sticks to Mark's order, and vise-versa
- Ninety percent of the content of Mark is found in Matthew, and more than 50% is found in Luke. In the case of the triple tradition, the common material (usually called a pericope, which means an individual unit of a story that forms a complete thought, story, or teaching) that is similar verbatim is about 50%.
- Mark is closer to both Matthew and Luke than they are to each other
- Matthew and Luke themselves have common material that is otherwise non-existent in Mark. Some material is similar verbatim and some are similar very little. This material is called the double tradition. [^9]
- Agreement between Matthew and Luke begins where Mark begins, or ends where Mark ends

These and some more basic facts beg for explanation. The simplest and the most universally accepted explanation of these facts is that of literary dependence, in that its either the gospels were directly copying from one to the other, or that they all depended on a single source they all used, which brings us to the Synoptic Problem.

The Synoptic Problem is a problem that puzzled gospel scholarship for more than a century. If it indeed is the case that the gospel were interdependent on each other, how would that interdependence work. Does Matthew use Mark, or does Mark summarize Matthew? Does Luke rely on both of them, or did Matthew and Luke independently draw from a common, lost source? This is basically the entire issue of the Synoptic Problem.

#### The Two-Source Hypothesis

Among the hypothesizes that have been put forward by scholars to explain the literary dependence of the synoptic gospels, this hypothesis has been the most popular and the most widely agreed upon.

The argument for this hypothesis goes like this

1. Large percentage of Mark exists both in Matthew and Luke. And Matthew and Luke are both significantly larger than Mark. Its really improbable that Mark copied Matthew and Luke, since if that is the case we would have to conclude that Mark disregarded so much good material in his supposed sources. We should rather conclude that **Matthew and Luke copied Mark**.
2. In the triple tradition (where all three of them agree ), Matthew and Luke agree only when Mark starts telling that story. Had they known each other, they would have agreed against Mark. Adding the fact that they start and stop agreeing where Mark starts and stops, it is fair to conclude that they copied Mark independently, meaning **Matthew and Luke didn't copy each other**.
3. If Mark can be accounted for the material in all three gospels, the double tradition — the common material between Matthew and Luke but not Mark— is left unaccounted for. Since we already established that Matthew and Luke did not copy each other, it stands to reason that they both relied on an earlier single source. This source has usually been referred to as "Q", which stands for "_Quelle_", a German word for "source".

In nutshell then, it has been a long standing consensus of biblical studies that the gospels were highly interdependent, and that this interdependence can be explained by the priority of the Gospel of Mark, its usage as source by Matthew and Luke, and the existence of another source material which explains the common material that exists in Matthew and Luke.

It should, however, be noted that this does not mean that this is the last word about the Synoptic Problem. There are many scholars who reject some or all of the conclusions proposed in the Two-Source hypothesis, for multiple problems that can be found in it. And I would suggest anybody who is interested in this area of study dig deep into all the hypothesis and the problems in each.

## Conclusion on the Composition of the Bible

Starting from the last piece, we have, so far, been discussing the scholarly positions on the composition of some of the books of the Bible. On the last one, we saw the composition of the Pentateuch from many hypothetical sources and of the Book of Isaiah from different people living in different time. Here we discussed the authorship and composition of the synoptic gospels. If you have been following up closely, you would have probably noticed an interesting trend, which is the non-existence of a single, isolated genius. I have tried to point this out briefly earlier and I will repeat it again. Ancient mind worked very different compared to how the modern mind works. Prior to the proliferation of Hellenic civilization in the fourth century BCE, the notion of author was pretty much non-existent. Interestingly, there isn't even a word that could easily be translated to "author" in Ancient Hebrew. What we have for people who write is the word "scribe" translated in to English. And scribe wasn't like how we in the modern world imagine it to be. They won't just copy their material, like a machine. They frequently engage in interpretation, redaction and et cetera, in order to adapt the material they are working with to their respective community.
