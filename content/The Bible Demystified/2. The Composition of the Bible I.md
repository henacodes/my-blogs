It was afternoon 1893, Washington D.C. A man , by the name Charles Augustus Briggs, was put on a Presbyterian General Assembly trial for his, rather unorthodox, teachings he uttered about the nature of the Bible, history, prophesy and the church on his inauguration speech two years earlier at Union Theological Seminary. Among other things that made him put on a trial, his rejection of the traditionally held belief —that the author the Pentateuch (the first five books of the Bible: Genesis, Exodus, Numbers, Leviticus and Deuteronomy) is Moses—was a significant one . [^1]

It does feel quite fringe and counter-intuitive thing to suggest that Moses did not write the Pentateuch. After all, 80% of the content is story of how he [Moses] got the people of Israel out of Egypt and how he received commandments directly from God. But this doesn't do justice to the countless problems that come along with presupposing that Moses or any other single person wrote them. Not to mention the fact that we have no attestation from the text itself about who wrote it. Even though authorial anonymity is anomalous in the modern world, it was a mundane literary practice in the ancient world, particularly in pre-Hellenistic ages. However, the fact that people wrote anonymously doesn't mean that the presupposition that Moses wrote is justified. It just means we are, by design, unable to know, beyond an educated speculation, who specifically wrote them.

This also raises another question. If indeed the authorship of the Pentateuch can be doubted, what about other books of the Bible? What reasonable justification do the people who deny traditional authorship attribution , such as Charles Briggs, give to their position?

## The Genesis of Genesis

Joseph, the son of Jacob and great-grandson of the patriarch Abraham, is said to have had a dream where the sun and the moon along with 11 stars bow down to one star; the sun, the moon and the 11 stars represent his father, mother and eleven brothers respectively. This is Joseph foretelling his future rise to power in Egypt and the eventual submission of his family to his authority.

Joseph is the eleventh child of the twelve children of Jacob, 12 of them being men who are the eponymous ancestors of the 12 tribes of Israel. By the time Joseph had that dream, he was still young man compared to his older brothers. He lived with his father at home, and they lived in the far wilds herding. Motivated by their jealousy and resentment over Joseph's dream—understandable reaction given the ancient people's strong social hierarchy based on birth order— they plot to do something about this problematic younger brother. Genesis 37 is where we get how the plot went down.

One day, Jacob, the father, tells Joseph to go visit his brothers in the wild and take some food with him along the way, and Joseph does that. When his brothers see Joseph coming from afar towards them, they realize that this is the perfect time to get rid of him. So they agree to kill him and tell their father that a ferocious animal devoured him. But one of them, Judah , comes with this idea. Instead of them killing Joseph, they just sell him to the merchants (Ishmaelites), thereby avoiding killing their own brother while also getting rid of his seemingly cocky dreams.

Its all fun and games until we start reading the narrative closely, paying attention to every minute detail. Once we start doing that, we immediately run into quite lots of repetitions, awkward transitions, apparent gaps and contradictions. Some of these can actually be explained by authorial style. But there are also some of those that can not reasonably be explained by anything. For example, the text wildly contradicts itself on who actually rescues Joseph and takes him to Egypt, alternating between the Ishmaelites and the Midianites. In the very scene Judah proposes selling Joseph to the Ishmaelites, Midianite traders passing by pull Joseph out of the pit independently. This leaves Reuben, the oldest sibling, shocked to find the pit empty, yet a few verses later, it is the Midianites who sell Joseph into Egypt. These narrative inconsistencies, writes Joel Baden,

> derive from the narrative presence and action of both Ishmaelites and Midianites in the sale of Joseph. When we read the text as it stands, according to its plain meaning, these two foreign groups are the source of great confusion. The Ishmaelite traders arrive on the scene first (v. 25), leading Judah to persuade his brothers that rather than kill Joseph, they ought to sell him; they will be just as effectively rid of him, and even profit in the process (vv. 26–27). Before the transaction can take place, however, the Midianite traders pass by, and though it was the brothers who had planned to sell Joseph to the caravan of Ishmaelites, it is the Midianites, according to the plain reading of the text, who pull Joseph from the pit, and it is the Midianites who sell him to the Ishmaelites. 4 The Midianites, therefore, appear to frustrate Judah’s plans, as it is they who reap the benefits of selling Joseph to the Ishmaelites. If the brothers are present for this, they are strikingly silent. Far more difficult is the notice at the end of v.28 that the Ishmaelites, upon purchasing Joseph, brought him to Egypt. This is expected in light of what preceded in v.25, where the Ishmaelites are said to be heading toward Egypt. But it is expressly contradicted by what follows at the end of the chapter in v. 36: “The Midianites, meanwhile, sold him in Egypt to Potiphar.” If this were not problem enough, Genesis 39:1 states that Potiphar bought Joseph “from the Ishmaelites who had brought him there.” [^2]

About 30 chapters back from the narrative of Joseph's lore, we find the flood narrative, the one where God decided to swipe the entire earth life from existence and rescue only Noah and his extended family, along with female and male animals of every kind. " In 6:17–22, God tells Noah that he is going to bring a flood and instructs him to bring into the ark two of each kind of animal; we are then told that 'Noah did so; just as God had commanded him, so he did' (v. 22). In 7:1–5, Yahweh tells Noah that he is going to bring a flood and instructs him to bring into the ark two of each unclean animal and seven pairs of every clean animal; we are then told that 'Noah did just as Yahweh commanded him' (v. 5). The story thus presents the same events happening twice—God’s announcement of the flood, instructions about the animals, and the fulfillment of those instructions by Noah—which marks it as a doublet. The story also tells us that on the one hand, Noah is to bring two of every animal (and he does so), and on the other, that he is to bring two of every unclean and seven of every clean animal (and he does so)—a glaring contradiction."[^3]

The aforementioned and many other contradictions, doublets and even triplets that we find throughout the Pentateuch were the major reasons why the scholars abandoned the traditional authorial ascription to Moses, and have long since posited the hypothesis named the Documentary Hypothesis. This theory suggests that there were four sources, usually named J ( after the divine name Yahweh; the German transliteration starts with J), E ( after the divine name Elohim ), P ( short for Priest ), D ( short for Deuteronomy), which were used in combination by a redactor who gave us the current form of the Pentateuch.

The hypothesis [^4] has gone through huge revisions throughout the last century since the work of the pioneering scholar Julius Wellhausen ( a Lutheran minster by the way ), the latest of which is called the Neo-Documentary hypothesis, which tries to acknowledge the many criticisms that have been leveled up against the hypothesis and gets rid of the weakness while maintaining the four-sources hypothesis. There are also other scholarly hypothesis, such as the Supplementary Hypothesis and the Fragmentary hypothesis, which try to explain the evidently multiple-sourced books of the Pentateuch. While different on the number of sources, on the date of the composition of the sources and many other major/minor issues, scholars of the Hebrew Bible are generally in consensus agreement over the existence of the sources P, D and non-P, where non-P is the source proponents of the documentary hypothesis identify to be the other two sources; J and E. [^5]

What we can understand from all this is that the Pentateuch never had a single phase of development with a single person behind all of it. Rather it had multiple people involved in its composition with multiple historical and rhetorical context behind the people involved.

## The Prophets and their Scribes

Satan, the malevolent and adversarial creature who always marches against the kingdom of God, is popularly believed to have formerly been an angel. "Upon rebelling against God and trying to make himself His equal, he was thrown down from the heavens to earth, along with his allies. He used to be the most glorious of all angels, or so says the Christian tradition, that he was called the Morning Star.

But, may we ask, where is this story of Satan coming from? Well this story is one that we find in the pages of the book of Isaiah chapter 14, which reads:

> <sup>12</sup> How you are fallen from heaven, O Day Star, son of Dawn! How you are cut down to the ground, you who laid low the nations! <sup>13</sup>You said in your heart, ‘I will ascend to heaven, above the stars of God. I will set my throne on high ... <sup>15</sup> Instead, you are brought down to Sheol, to the bottom of the Pit.

Although the Christian tradition has always attributed this to be referring to Satan, scholars of Isaiah have long realized that this narrative is specifically about a King of the Babylonian empire, since verse 4 reads: "On the day the LORD has given you rest from your pain and sorrow and from hard service, 4 you will take up this taunt against the king of Babylon." It should be noted that there is a major scholarly debate on whether or not the original song (yea, this chapter is a song) was first composed to be used against the Assyrian king.

But wait a minute? Didn't the prophet Isaiah who was supposed to utter this oracle live long before the reign of Babylonian yoke and during the Assyrian reign? and the answer is yes, which brings us to the main concern I want to raise. The book of Isaiah is a composition of three different prophetic sources, one of which directly comes from the oracles of the Isaiah of Jerusalem. Chapters 1 through 39 contain (not necessarily wholly ) the prophecies as uttered by Isaiah, during the reign of Assyrian empire (lets call this portion First Isaiah ) while chapters 40 to 55 were composed by later scribes living in the sixth century Babylonian exile, which we shall refer to as Deutro-Isaiah. The last chapters spanning chapter 56 through 66 (Trito-Isaiah) were composed by scribes living in post-exilic time under the shadow of the Persian empire, the time when the people were allowed to come back to their land from exile and restart their lives again.

### A Little Bit of History

Let us cover some of the most important bits of history of the nation of Israel before we go into the literary history of the Book of Isaiah.

King David is arguably the most popular king many of us would be familiar with. So much so that his name is one of the most frequently used names in the modern world. Following the reigns of David and his successor Solomon, the Davidic Dynasty split into two: the Northern (Ephraimite) Kingdom and the Southern (Judahite) Kingdom.

Around the eighth century, both kingdoms along with fellow small kingdoms of the Levant (Syria, Israel, Judah, Philistia) had enjoyed relative autonomy because the Assyrian (located at modern day northern Iraq) Empire was militarily weak and was busy with internal civil wars. Unfortunately in 745 BCE the king Tiglath-Pileser III came to rule over the Assyrian Empire and had expansionist plans. By the end of the century the northern kingdom was conquered and made Assyrian province.

Over a century after the campaigns of the expansionist king, another superpower, Babylon (located at central and southern Iraq), came to prominence and defeated the Assyrian rule around late seventh century. The new Babylonian power eventually made its way to the southern kingdom (Judah) and annexed it by 586 BCE. They destroyed the Temple, and took the then king away, blinding him after he had seen his sons executed. Judah ceased to exist as a state, though many people naturally remained in the Land, with a civil servant. This is the time we usually refer to as Babylonian Exile.

Yet another power grew prominent, out of Persia (located at Iran) ruled by King Cyrus. He defeated the Babylonian power and annexed all the provinces under the latter's rule, including the southern kingdom. But fortunately for the exiled Jews Cyrus allowed and even supported them go back to their Homeland and rebuild their temple in Jerusalem. This is the time we would generally refer to as Post-exilic time or sometimes Second Temple.

### Coming back

In this span of history, the historical prophet Isaiah was active around the time the Assyrian Empire conquered the northern kingdom, at least up until 701 BCE.[^7] While it is true that many parts of the First Isaiah do go back to the historical Isaiah, there are many other parts of the First and the proceeding sections that do not make sense when looked at through the lens of eighth century audience. One of those being the chapter 14 narrative we looked at earlier, which contains the taunt that the king of Babylon is like the god of the morning star (in Latin, lucifer), who fell from heaven. Based on this observation we may conclude that "the prediction of the fall of Babylon in 13–14 is very unlikely to go back to Isaiah himself". [^8]

In regard to the parts of Isaiah other than first Isaiah, Barton notes "everything after chapter 40 makes sense only in the period when Jews in exile looked to the arrival of Cyrus II, the Persian king, to overcome Babylon and give permission for a resettlement of the Land . Cyrus is referred to by name in 44:28 and 45:1 (consecutive verses), and chapters 46–7 present a lengthy taunt against the Babylonians, deriding their gods as nothing but objects of wood and stone that have to be carted around on ‘weary animals’, and contrasting them with Yahweh, who on the contrary ‘carries’ Israel."[^9] We can understand from this that the scribes of both Deutro-Isaiah and Trito-Isaiah were writing to adapt and adopt the former oracles of the prophet Isaiah to their respective historical circumstance each found themselves in.

---

To many of you the idea that the prophet did not write a book bearing his name may come as a surprise. But that is only because of the staggering difference between the modern notion of authorship and the one in the ancient world. Our modern notion of authorship is very much influenced by other cultural and philosophical phenomena such as the concept of intellectual rights, copyright and things related, which were non-existent back then.

## Conclusion

In this piece article, I have tried to briefly go over the complex and elaborate process that was instrumental in the composition of the Hebrew Bible as we have it today. One of the most important lesson we can take away from the historical development of the Bible is that the Bible is no monolithic and static book. It is a multi-generational book spanning generations of different stripes and is a product of dynamic and continuous conversations through those generations. The subsequent generations adopted and adapted it to drive their theological, political and rhetorical goals home.
