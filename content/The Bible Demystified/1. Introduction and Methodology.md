The Bible is a literature that has influenced the world more than any other literary work; it was, and is, a source of union and division, of conflict and peace, and of piety and impiety. Many have used it to justify slavery, just as others did to abolish it. Countless others went to great lengths to establish unity between individuals and groups, while others used it to preach their sectarian rhetoric, not to mention the millions of others who edify and nourish their spiritual lives while some deconstruct their faith and abandon their religious affiliation entirely. This article isn't about how people use the Bible, nor is it a value judgment about the same. Rather, the aforementioned cases are serve as clear demonstrations of how the Bible has a direct effect on our day-to-day lives and our interactions with the world around us. This, in turn, I hope, is a good reason to motivate you to consider understanding what the Bible is and what it "really says" (ignore the scare quotes for now, please. will you?).

Provided I convinced you for why you should consider studying what the Bible is and what it says, let us now take a bird's eye view of what the bible really is.

## The Bible doesn't exist

Yes, you heard that right: there is no such thing as "The Bible" in a sense that denotes a single, universal list of books for all Christians today. (The number and the list are not necessarily the same; we shall see this when discussing the canon.) The Protestant tradition famously considers the Bible to be a library of 66 books. The Roman Catholic Church, however, recognizes 46 books in the Old Testament compared to the Protestant 39, bringing its total to 73 books. Our own Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church has at its disposal a whopping 81 books.

So, when one speaks about "the Bible," they are essentially saying, "the library of books my tradition considers to be Holy Scripture." We could complicate this further, but suffice it to say that every tradition has its own line demarcating what is and isn't considered part of scripture. Of course, the Bible, in the barest sense of the term, does exist; however, we should keep in mind that two people may not be referring to the same set of books when they utter the word. In this series of articles, when I use the term "Bible," I am referring to the set of scriptures Christians consider, or have considered, holy and binding in their respective communities. I am not endorsing any specific canon from any tradition. Whenever I speak of "the Bible," I am most likely referring to characteristics shared by all canons. Please do let me know if you find any instances where I have inadvertently assumed a specific canon (likely the Protestant one, given my background) and I will be happy to correct it. You will find an extended discussion on the issue of canon in subsequent installments.

## The Problem of Methodology

Biblical studies, in the sense of simply studying the Bible, is practiced in many circles. The one most people are familiar with takes place within churches for devotional and theological purposes. The other occurs in academic circles, where the Bible is studied at high-level, and often secular universities, such as the famous Yale Divinity School or Princeton Theological Seminary. Before proceeding to the methodological differences between the two, I should mention that it is quite difficult to draw a clear line between them. As is often the case with conceptual categories, there are many instances where it is hard to categorize a specific study circle as residing strictly in one camp or the other. With that said, there is a stark methodological difference between these circles, which results in them reaching astronomically different conclusions, even when they are reading the very same text.

### Methodology of Church Circles

One of the things that characterizes churches of all stripes is that each has a distinct set of doctrines, whether formally confessed or not, which they believe to be indispensable for the inclusion and exclusion of members. One of the primary subjects of these doctrinal convictions is the Bible itself. These confessions regarding the nature of the Bible are not merely expressions of cognitive belief; rather, they go far beyond simple creedal adherence and actively dictate how the Bible is read. I will now list some of these doctrines and discuss how they inform one’s interpretation of the text.

**"We should read the Bible in the expectation that what we find there will be true"**
In the 1970s, a group of conservative evangelical pastors (around 200, if I am not mistaken) gathered and agreed upon a series of articles of faith. These individuals— (yes I will use em-dash, what are you gonna do about it ) who strike me as people who may have never taken a single verse of the Bible seriously enough to read it carefully—established a binding set of doctrines for many (perhaps most) Baptist churches in America at the time. This would later become a heavy burden for many honest and faithful readers of the Bible. In these articles, they state that...

> Being wholly and verbally God-given, Holy Scripture is without error or fault in all its teaching, no less in what it states about God's acts in creation, about the events of world history, and about its own literary origins under God, than in its witness to God's saving grace in individual lives.[^1]

Of course, it is obvious that anyone who has spent even a fraction of their time trying to learn something about the Bible would know that this is an utterly stupid and false statement. Why I say this will become clear as we go through subsequent articles in this series, but I digress (for now).

Even many Christians who do not strictly subscribe to this specific statement would agree that the Bible should be read as true rather than false. The truth it contains may sometimes be seen as poetic or symbolic rather than strictly factual—this is particularly the case for more "liberal" Christians—but for them, it is still not an option to suggest that anything in the Bible is an expression of error.

**"Scripture is to be read as relevant"**
In his letter to the church of Corinth, Paul orders women to cover their heads while praying. Even though this is still practiced in all traditional churches (Catholics, Orthodox churches), this is a highly contested issue in many Pentecostal churches today. While the people in favor of women covering their heads in church quote the 1st Corinthians passage, the other side of the aisle—who are usually heard saying that it’s really not a problem for women not to cover their heads anymore—say that Paul here was addressing a specific circumstance in a specific context which needed Paul's commandment. The people in favor rebut their opponents' argument by retreating to a more general issue of the relevance of biblical passages. They would go like: "Suggesting that this passage is supposed to be applied in the specific circumstance of the church of Corinth and not our time today, is a clear opposition to the fact that the Bible is the eternal word of God with no time boundary." The discussion goes on.

It’s exactly the issue of relevance that I would like to bring here. By many, and maybe all Christians today, it’s believed that the Bible's relevance is forever, and that even if there are passages that don't seem to have any relevance to our current lives at all, it is our task, as readers of Scripture, to discern what God is saying to us through the inclusion of such passages in the Bible.

**"Scripture is self-consistent. The reader must not play one part of the Bible off against another"**
Up until the middle of my teenage years, I was a staunch reader of the patriarchal narratives (stories of Abraham and his descendants in Genesis) along with the Deuteronomistic History (the stories spanning the book of Judges up to the book of Kings). The founding father to whom the entire history of Israel owes its origin was nobody but Abraham. And there is this story of Abraham in Genesis where God commands him (Abraham), as a test, to sacrifice his precious little boy to him. Reading that story was a challenging task for me back then, as it is for many readers of the Bible. The first and the most glaring one being the idea that God would suggest a human sacrifice, even as a test. No less concerning is the idea that God would test people when he is omniscient and knows everything, which is the one I wanted to bring here. (ignore the banal philosophical concerns )

I have learned in different instances of church sermons that God doesn't test us and that we will only find ourselves being tested when we let ourselves go in pursuit of evil pleasure, as James taught (James 1:13). So my mind always wondered, "If God doesn't test us in any way as James made it clear, why is Abraham being tested here? Wait, can and does the Bible contain conflicting ideas about God, or anything for that matter?" I am sure as hell many of you relate to this story in one way or another.

Indeed, the question of whether or not the Bible can contain competing theologies and accounts of history is one of the things that keeps many readers awake at night. But many of today's Christians are on the side that strongly affirms that the Bible does not and cannot contain any conflicting theological claims. Some may allow for contradictory historical narratives (by accounting for scribal errors and other common apologetic rationalizations). But theological contradictions, like the one I mentioned above (it should be noted that I am not necessarily saying the Bible is contradictory on that specific issue), are off the table. This theological commitment to the univocality [^2] of the Bible in theological issues is essentially the result of the first commitment I mentioned above; the one that says that the Bible should be read in the expectation that what we find there is true. Suggesting that one part of the Bible is conflicting with another one would by definition render at least one of the two passages erroneous.

#### Presuppositions and Interpretation

As I mentioned earlier, the many theological commitments people have about the Bible itself serve as more than just mere adherence. They are instrumental in guiding the interpretative endeavors of the Bible; they reflect one's interest as to why they are reading the Bible. When somebody who holds on to the doctrines I mentioned above comes to read, for example, the many elaborate passages of genealogies in Genesis and the book of Chronicles, or the confusingly erotic book the Song of Songs, they aren't just trying to absorb names of a bunch of people and their descent or the relationship between Solomon and the lady. Instead, they are continuesly trying to discern what God is trying to communicate through these narratives.

When some stumble upon contradictions, both in historical and theological regard, they would have to employ different interpretative techniques which would allow for both passages to be true. More often than not, they would resort to two options. One is to prioritize the interpretation [they had before realizing the apparent contradiction] of one of the passages and subjugate the other passage to the former by reinterpreting it. The other is by laying an entirely foreign interpretive framework on the passages, which often has all the bells and whistles to allow for (or eliminate) the apparent inconsistency. We will hopefully have a dedicated discussion on the history of biblical interpretation moving forward.

### Methodology of Secular Academy

As I noted earlier, it is very troublesome to suggest that any and all academies teaching the Bible can be categorized as either theological or secular. But we can have pretty recurrent characteristics that we see in one and not the other. For example, theologically committed seminaries usually make both teachers and students sign a statement of faith affirming what they consider to be orthodox confessions. Two of my favorite Bible scholars, Peter Enns and Mike Licona, were both shunned from their respective colleges where they were teaching, because of their "unorthodox" positions on some issues.[^3] Yea, it’s hard finding the dividing line. But don't come tell me such colleges firing professors are secular in any way, shape, or form.

The recurring characteristic of secular academies that teach the Bible is the treatment of the Bible as a library of literary works which are products of their own time and place. In this school of endeavor, contrary to the theological/"traditional" one, one part of the Bible is not expected to be in full agreement with another; neither is it expected to be relevant to the people participating in the study. The ideal goal here is solely to interrogate and understand the Bible in its own historical and cultural context. Hence why it has been usually referred to as the "historical-critical method."

#### Problems of Historical-Critical Method

Even though the historical-critical method is the guiding principle in my goal to demystify the Bible, its development throughout the centuries is nothing short of a controversial and complex philosophical qualm. The Enlightenment philosophers, such as Baruch Spinoza, are notable examples who influenced and pioneered the discipline. As Enlightenment philosophy is characterized by deistic inclinations, presuppositions—such as the idea that miracles do not happen—were the assumptions that dictated some of the conclusions the said philosophers reached when they studied the Bible. This problem is something that is also seen in some contemporary biblical scholars, where they cast doubt on the biblical literature that they otherwise wouldn't for other ancient literary works.

I want to clarify one misconception that I think is probably building up (or has already built up) in the minds of many readers by now. Even if it’s indeed true that anti-supernaturalism and a rejection of miracles influenced the conclusions many scholars reached in the past, it is completely misguided to suggest that the entire project of the historical-critical approach to the Bible presupposes these things in order to arrive at some of the most widely agreed-upon theories. In point of fact, many of the scholars—who proposed theories many conservative evangelicals believe to be the result of anti-supernaturalist presumptions—are indeed ordained ministers of different church traditions.

With that out of the way, I hope this series of articles will intrigue and motivate you into digging deeper into the complex origins of the Bible.

Enjoy :D
