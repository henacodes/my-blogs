The question of canon has been one of the most controversial and a topic of intense inter-denominational debate a for long time. Funny enough, it is one of those topics highly debated in the scholarly circles too, but for different reasons from those driving debates between Protestant and Tewahedo Christians. If you have been reading the articles sequentially, you should be well aware by now that different sects of Christians have their own respective lists of books they consider holy. Even before these Christian collections took shape, the Jewish community had solidified the Hebrew Bible canon into the 24 books of the Tanakh (the Torah, Nevi'im, and Ketuvim which translates to the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings respectively), which corresponds content-wise to the later Protestant Old Testament. As we have established already, Protestants have 66 books in their Bible, the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church has 81 (with a nuance here), the Roman Catholic Church has 73, and the Eastern Orthodox has 76. As this article is written purely ( at least I would like to think so) from an academic point of view, it absolutely doesn't purport to vindicate or denigrate any canon of any sect. The purpose of this installment is merely to explore how our modern Bible got be the way it is right now.

---

The word canon comes from the Greek 'κανών', which refers, in literary scholarship, to classical standards of literary productions that became models for subsequent writers. The term, but, means a specified collection of books that make up the Bible, in today's world. The central question that intrigued, and is intriguing, scholars of the Bible for quite a while is that: How exactly did we get the specified list of books we call the Bible now? What was the concept of "Scriptures" and "Canon" in early Judaism and Christianity? What were the driving forces that participated in determining the current sets of canon we have these days?

## The Notions of Scripture and Canon

Broadly speaking, "scriptures" is usually used to refer a to set of books some religious groups believe to have come from their god in some sense or to express the will of their god. This idea of scriptures seems to have stemmed from a much earlier belief in a “heavenly book” that contains divine wisdom and knowledge as well as divine decrees by which adherents order their lives. This notion has parallels in ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt, where a heavenly book not only indicated the future plans of God, but also the destinies of human beings. We can find sentiments of "heavenly book" in the Hebrew Bible where Moses pleads to God to forgive the sins of Israelite or "blot him out of the book of life." But God said to Moses, ‘Whoever has sinned against me I will blot out of my book’  (Exod 32:32–33). Similarly, we find in the New Testament the Apostle Paul referring to his colleagues as those “whose names are in the book of life” (Phil 4:3). Interestingly, the Qur’an also speaks of a divine book of destinies in which “no misfortune strikes on earth or in yourselves without its being [written] in a Book before we cause it to be” (Surah 57.22).

### Scriptures as "Defiling the Hands"

The Jews—some time in ancient, certainly before common era— used to follow this practice of putting foods for offering (i.e sacrifice) along with their sacred writings. But mice would come and eat not only the food, but also the scriptures. And the sages later declared the writings things that would make hands unclean, "with perhaps the hope that this conclusion would make Jews change the place where they stored them, i.e., away from the sacrificial foods, and the writings would thus be saved from destruction."[^1]

In late first and early second century, rabbis started using the phrase "defile hands" to refer to their sacred scriptures. This refers to rendering the person handling the sacred texts ritually unclean. This seems to stem from the fact that the Ark of Covenant made people who come in contact ritually unclean, sometime as far as killing them ((2 Sam 6:2–8). Only the priests were allowed to touch the Ark, and anybody who who isn't eligible will be rendered unclean when they come in contact with it. "It appears that the touching of the Ark by those unqualified to do so adversely affects them and so does touching sacred scripture by the unqualified."

### What About Canon?

Just about a year ago, I was taking freshman Anthropology course. And one of the most recurrent features I saw while reading my course materials was how many of the words we take for granted are hard to pin down and define precisely. Well, the same is true of the word canon. I earlier gave you a definition that captures the meaning as we use the word today. But as we go back in time and study antique conceptualization, defining the words becomes really hard.

Athanasius of Alexandria inaugurated the long history of using the term "canon" to refer to a fixed list or catalogue of the church’s sacred and authoritative scriptures, in his famous 39th Festal Letter (a letter he sent annually to announce the date of Easter). In addition to talking about just Easter, Athanasius identified the writings that could be read in the churches and he referred to them as a “canon.” Interestingly, the recognition of fixed list collection of scriptures began in the second century for rabbinic Jews and in the third and fourth centuries for the churches in regard to their NT canon.

Before Athanasius however, the term referred generally to “rule of faith”, the rules/authorities the Christian tradition considered to be identifiers of the faith and its mission. We even have the term used in New Testament four times, — "the first three describe Paul’s description of the limits or sphere of his mission and his aim not to build on the work of others (2 Cor 10:13, 15, 16), and the final use is in regard to the rule of faith (the essence of the cross) to be followed in the churches (Gal 6:16)." [^2] From this we can clearly see the difference in usage of the term "canon" before and after Athanasius, where the modern usage pretty much aligns with his usage.

### Canon of the Antiquity

The ancient world was filled with canons (guides, models, regulations) left and right. There was a canon for every single sphere of activity, be it painting, literature or music. The Egyptians had canons of art by which the artisans were guided in their craft. Canons of art from the Egyptian Kingdom include the uniform shape of human figures, skin color (women were uniformly given a lighter skin color than men), and arms crossed over the chest to indicate the death of the person portrayed. In another instance, the notion of a standard was also employed in philosophy in ancient Greek as the criterion or canon by which one discovers what is true and false. Epicurus argued that logic and method conceptually stem from "canon" by which one could know what was true or false and thus whether it was worth investigating. Not only that, but "Homer and Hesiod were considered widely revered among the Greeks and used as standards or models for literary writings. Homer’s writings functioned as a supreme authority among the Greeks and in a very real sense became canon par excellence for them" [^3]

The grammarians of the great Library of Alexandria of Egypt employed the older conceptualization of canon( which is again guide or rule) when curating their library, even though they didn't technically use the word 'canon' per se. They acknowledged written canons that were practiced in reference to grammar and the literary models that subsequent writers were advised to follow. Among the classical literary works that they thought would be canonical for succeeding generation are works of Homer,Hesiod, Sophocles, Plato, Aristotle, Aristophanes, Herodotus, Thucydides, and Aesop. Those who departed from these standards were often criticized for doing so or ignored. [^4]

### Jewish Notion of Canon

Here is something you need to remember when thinking about the history of canon. Speaking about a fixed list of sacred scriptures either for Jews or Christians prior to the end of first century or the beginning of the second century is very problematic, and what historians call an anachronism. Imagine watching Jesus from The Chosen TV show using smartphone or him talking about airplanes? That does not make sense, does it?! In the same sense, to talk about a fixed list of books Jews of the first century had is awkward and devoid of historical evidence. It's only after the late first century or early second century where the idea of fixed and closed list of books became a thing.

#### Scriptures of the Second Temple Judaism

Second Temple Judaism is the phrase we use to refer to the time between the reconstruction of the Jewish Temple under the Persian hegemony around the 6th century BCE and the destruction of the very Temple in 70 CE. The question is what can we speak about the scriptures of the Judaism that existed in this span of time.

One thing you have to keep in mind when speaking about Antique Judaism ( or even modern Judaism for that matter ) is that it wasn't a single monopolistic religion to which all Jews around the world must subscribe to. Jews not infrequently disagreed with each other on a variety of issues, including the nature and mode of life after death, whether there was life after death, and the books they acknowledged as Scripture. Judaism of the first century or before was a mixed bag of different forms of piety. This is also true in the Scriptures they recognized and that were read in their gatherings. So far as we can tell, all Jewish sects in the first century, including early Christianity, acknowledged the authority and inspiration of the books of Moses (the Pentateuch), even if the Samaritans’ Pentateuch differed in several respects from the Jewish Pentateuch acknowledged by both Jews and Christians.

To demonstrate the point a bit more, we can discuss the scriptures of Essenes. Essenes were sectarian groups that existed from second century BCE to late first century CE, who lived around the Dead Sea in what today is called Khirbet Qumran and are famous for producing the Dead Sea Scrolls. These people copied, transmitted, and also produced religious literature that sheds considerable light on their beliefs around the turn of the common era. . Several books that were not included in the later Hebrew Bible of Judaism ( or Old Testament of Protestant Canon ) were nonetheless acknowledged as sacred and authoritative religious texts among the Essenes. Meanwhile the Essenes seem to have excluded books, such as the Book of Esther, that later made it to Hebrew Bible. They, for example, copied 1 Enoch, Jubilees (መጽሐፈ ኩፋሌ) , Tobit (መጽሐፈ ጦቢት), and Sirach (መጽሐፈ ሲራክ) along with whats is called Temple Scroll. While the fact that we found copied of book doesn't conclusively indicate what they included and excluding in their religious piety, partly because we may have yet to discover all of their literary productions, it speaks volumes to what they prioritized and adored.

While it is true that the Jewish community waited up until the late first and early second to even discuss fixed list of canon, we should note that the two categories of their scriptures, namely the Law (Torah) and the Prophets (Nevi'im), were pretty much close to stable. It is in the third category the Writings (Ketuvim) that much of the fluidity is. It sometimes functioned as a catch-all phrase that denote all books that weren't included in the first two categories.

#### Myth of the Council At Jamnia

For about a century or so, biblical scholars taught that the Jews officially closed the third part of their biblical canon, the Writings, at a supposed Council at Jamnia. However this hypothesis has long been rejected by later scholars. Following the catastrophic destruction of the Temple at Jerusalem around 70 CE, the Jewish sages started wondering how a religion based largely on temple sacrifice and priesthood can now survive without its core element of piety. It was less of an official and universal council and more of group of people who are met with existential crisis. [^5]

#### The Emergence of 22/24 Books Canon

Right around the end of the first century, two texts emerged from which we find the notion that the Jewish scriptures consist strictly of 22 or 24 books. Even though neither text clearly lists the presumed 22 or 24 books, it may say something about the growing concern among Jews to delineate their authoritative scriptures, for whatever reasons there were.

**Josephus**
Josephus was a Jewish historian and advocate (apologist perhaps ) who lived through the destruction of the Temple as a direct eyewitness, because he was himself an army general in Jewish war of the late 60s CE that eventually culminated in the destruction of the Temple. He later surrendered to the Romans and ultimately lived in Rome till his death.

In his discourse against the Egyptian sophist Apion, Josephus purported to defend the Jewish people from the polemics of his Pagan counterpart. In his defense of Jewish people, Josephus wrote that Jews’ sacred Scriptures contained twenty-two books. He wrote

> we do not possess myriads of inconsistent books, conflicting with each other. Our books, those which are justly accredited,4 are but two and twenty, and contain the record of all time.[^6]

He proceeds to categorize them under three groups, where five of them are the books of Moses, and the books written between the death of Moses and Artaxerxes are categorized under the prophets, which are numbered thirteen. The last group which contains four books, so he says, contain hymns to God and precepts for the conduct of human life.

There are a couple of things to mention here which complicate Josephus' canon. The first is, as mentioned above, he never provided explicit list of his 22-books canon. We may say with confidence that his first group does refer to the Pentateuch and may infer to some degree of certainty what his second group contained. But we are left to guess what his third group contained. Another issue is the fact that Josephus uttered this in-between a polemic against another polemic. Josephus may have been using the number as a rhetorical tool to combat Apion's skepticism towards the Jewish history. As a result, his canon number may not have even been a popular among his Jewish contemporaries. As McDonald notes: "it is difficult to say that Josephus’ list ... carried the day or was even well known in the first century CE"[^7]

**The Apocalypse of Ezra**
The Apocalypse of Ezra, or simply 4 Ezra (AKA 2 Esdras) is a late first century Jewish writing that was highly regarde by early Christians. In it we read the story of Ezra, the 5th century Jewish priest, and the five scribes beeing filled with divine wisdom and producing 94 books upon Ezra's dictation. God the instructs Ezra how to categorize the 94 books, in which the 24 are to be read by all people, both "worthy" and "unworthy". The remaining 70 must be kept secret and delivered exclusively to the wise, because they contain the deeper "spring of understanding, the fountain of wisdom, and the river of knowledge". Similar to Josephus, the author here also doesn't enlist the books that add up to 24 or 70.

**A Significant Underlying Issue**
Other than the fact that neither Josephus's _Against Apion_ nor _4 Ezra_ enlist the books that comprise their canon, there is one more fundamental issue that both literature have in common. And that is the fact that their supposed 22/24 numbered scriptures are not random, but by design inspired from the number of Greek alphabet (which is 24) and the number of Hebrew alphabet (which is 22). It seems the numbers 22 and 24 had more relevance in their own right than the content of list people were making. This seems to have come from ancient Greeks who divided works of Homer according to the number of Greek alphabets. "Earlier, this practice can be seen in the division of some of the Psalms by letters in the Hebrew alphabet, especially Psalms 119, which is structured around the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet. This may be the precedent for Josephus’ selection of the number twenty-two for the number of sacred books for the Jews books to include." [^8]

## The Church Fathers on Old Testament Canon
Let us divide the church fathers geographically and briefly go through the canon of different church fathers. 

### The Eastern Fathers

**Melito of Sardis**:  Due to some confusion in his purview on the scope of biblical canon, Melito, who was active around middle to late second century, makes a trip to East, perhaps Jerusalem, to inquire  the number and names of books in Jewish scriptures. In this list, Melito identifies the books of the Christian OT and their order,49 though not in their usual order,. and he omits Esther and Nehemiah, adds Wisdom of Solomon. The fact that his canon differs from the one later recognized as the Hebrew Bible ( same as Protestant canon )  in rabbinic Judaism shows as that the later canon wasn't fully formed even in the second century.

**Origen** (185–254): Historian of the church Eusebius records in his Ecclesiatical History that Origen "set forth the catalogue of the sacred Scriptures of the Old Testament, writing somewhat as follows in these words: But it should be known that there are twenty-two canonical books, according to the Hebrew tradition; the same as the number of the letters of their alphabet' " (Hist. eccl. 6.25.1–2, LC). We should however note that Origen did not limit himself to the biblical canon of the Jews, nor did he reject the use of deuterocanonical literature. "In his Homily on Numbers, Origen recommends that a Christian’s intellectual diet should begin with Esther, Judith, Tobit, and Wisdom of Solomon before proceeding to the Psalms and the Gospels."[^9]

**Athanasius** ( ca. 367). Athanasius excluded some writings of the Apocrypha (Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Esther, Judith, Tobit) as well as some Christian Apostolic Fathers (Didache and Shepherd of Hermas), declaring that they were not canonical, but nevertheless were valuable for instructing new converts (“those who newly join us”) in piety. On the other hand, he included in his list of canonical Scriptures the Epistle of Jeremiah and Baruch. (Ep. fest. 39.3–4)

### Western Fathers

**Hilary of Poitiers** (ca. 315–367):  This father seems to follow Origen's 22-book canon. But nevertheless adds two extra books to his canon, namely, Tobit and Judith, in order to make a twenty-four-book biblical canon, which he believed followed the number of letters in the Hebrew alphabet.

**Augustine** (354–430): One of the most influential theologians and philosophers of the west, has in his canon of Old Testament forty-four books. He identifies each of the twelve Minor Prophets, but also includes Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Tobit, Esther, Judith, 1–2 Maccabees, Esther,Baruch, and the Epistle of Jeremiah (Augustine, De doctrina christiana 2.13).  Augustine's canon was influential and did win the day in the west. 

After reviewing the canons of  the aforementioned and some other church fathers, Lee Martin McDonald concludes: 

> The Eastern Church fathers usually opted for the shorter OT canon, generally following either the twenty-two or twenty-four-book biblical canon that they believed comprised the sacred Scriptures of their Jewish siblings. However, the contents of their lists of sacred Scriptures vary in the fringe areas (Baruch, Epistle of Jeremiah, and Esther especially) and in their sequence, but they all have most of the HB canon in common. Generally speaking, the Western church followed the lead of Augustine and included many of the apocryphal writings. What is not clear is why they accepted the apocryphal books but rejected most of the pseudepigraphal writings that initially informed the faith of the early Christians and possibly Jesus as well.[^10]
