A strongly nationalistic Scotsman, once, sat down with that day's paper, whose headline read "Sidecup Sex Maniac Strikes Again." Filled with shock and utter confidence, the Scotsman reacted: "No Scot would do such a thing", only to find out the next Sunday that the perpetrator was actually a Scotsman. Instead of admitting, in the face of blatant evidence, that some Scotsmen would actually commit such crime, he responds to the new evidence by saying "No true Scotsman would do such a thing!" This is a story from the philosopher Antony Flew's thought experiment demonstrating the informal fallacy known as the No True Scotsman Fallacy. [^1]

This fallacy is notoriously used by religious people. This line of reasoning is how people usually to maintain group boundaries and preserve a group's perceived ideological purity.  One of the clearest examples this fallacy is committed is in discourses of biblical interpretation. For example, when faced with the reality of history of people using the Bible to justify their bad practices such as slavery, many Christians like to resort to: "Well no true Christian would justify slavery with the Bible."

In this article, we shall discuss how the Bible has been utilized by subsequent generation during the 2nd temple Judaism, the Christian church, and by the Bible itself. We will do lots of things. but committing the No True Scotsman fallacy is not going to be one of them.

## The Bible about the Bible

"During my early teenage years, I used to discuss lots of biblical issues with my uncle, who was (sort of) theologically literate. One of the things he used to say that stayed with me for long was: "The Bible interprets itself. You may find a verse's interpretation in the next verse, in the next chapter or even in different book of the Bible." In retrospect, I think his advice  influenced how I read the Bible from then on. And I think it's fair to say that most Christians today do agree with this methodology of interpreting the Bible.

To my own surprise, I find myself —the apparently much better educated version of me—in agreement with this position. That is, I do believe the Bible does interpret itself. But for completely different reasons than one most Christians do believe for.

### Redactors as Interpreters

Earlier in this series of articles I talked about the Bible being a composite of different books, which are written by different people, for different people and in different times. But even that itself is an over-simplified description of rather obscure trajectory of development, where each book bags its own set of complexities. As I have noted in [[2. The Composition of the Bible I]], many books of the Bible—unlike the popular thinking that they had a linear trajectory of development— have behind them a lengthy history of evolution, where different editors, called redactors in scholarly circles, have introduced changes to different parts. But "while their work was carried out anonymously, traces of their activity can be demonstrated in many places in Old Testament writings."[^2]

Scribes were the main actors that played huge role in proliferation of the literary tradition of the ancient Near East. These scribes were associated in particular with the temples and royal courts. In the context of Israel, it was the Priests who were writing and recording literary works. On the other hand the Prophets had their own pupils "who collected their words and recorded them so that they would not be forgotten." But one caveat here that is important for our discussion is that the scribes did not limit themselves to the exact verbatim words of their master. Instead they adapted and adopted the material in order to make it relevant to situation they found themselves in. "The materials were expanded and transformed to meet new situations, often completely altering their message in the process. The result of this editing issued forth in the prophetic books as they exist today." [^3] And this, ladies and gentlemen, is what we are calling by "the Bible interprets the Bible". We do not mean to say that we can just take our interpretation of one Bible passage to interpret another passage, as my uncle suggested. The fundamental difference between what my uncle says, by extension many Christian say today, and what I am saying is that while the former is of prescriptive by nature, the latter, which is my position, is a descriptive one.

**The Invisible Isaiah**
We have already established in previous installments that Isaiah is a composition of oracles and narratives from three different times of periods, where the first part, chapters 1 to 39, contain the oracles of the prophet Isaiah from eighth century, and parts of the first part along with chapters 40 to 55 are composed by scribe(s) living in the sixth century living in Babylonian exile. The rest of is composed by another scribe(s) living in post-exilic age. You may wonder what redactors anything to do with biblical interpretation. But here is the thing. The exilic unknown prophet/redactor who had access the oracles of prophet Isaiah inserts his material in between the oracles of the original Isaiah. Threats against the Babylonian Empire (Isa 13; 14:3–21) were inserted right beside threats against the Assyrian Empire (Isaiah 14:24–27), the latter of which is contemporary to the original Prophet. As Reventlow notes: "The placement of this section leaves both the original intent of the earlier threat against Assyria unaltered, while making room for a new interpretation of this ancient prophetic word of doom. What once was true of the oppressive ruling superpower of Assyria, which had long since dissipated, continues to be valid analogously in the carrying out of a new political agenda against Israel. "[^4]


## Qumranic Hermeneutics 
On the previous installment where we discussed the development of canon, we  briefly mentioned the scriptures of the Qumran community. Before the mid 20th century little was known about these people. But the 1947 discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls, which was the production of the very people, shade significant light on the life and practice of the community and broadly the late second temple Judaism. 

Th Qumran community believed that they were living in the end times, and thought that every prophesy in the scriptures should be interpreted to signify the end times and in order to obtain what is meaningful for those now living. Interestingly, they believed that an end times interpretation of the prophets can be obtained even if the uttering prophet himself isn't aware of that specific interpretation. "In the Habakkuk Commentary (1QpHab) we find proclaimed the essential statement: “And God spoke to Habakkuk that he should write down what will come over the last generation. However, the consummation of this time he has not made known to him” (7:1–2). The prophet himself therefore still did not know what his proclamations should signify. Now in the present they can be interpreted and understood in their true sense."[^5] In this interpretive lens the original situation in  which the word of the prophet was spoken is consciously passed over. This kind of interpretation is called Pesher.

## The Origin of Allegory
“If the cattle or horses had hands, if they could paint with these hands…, then the gods would be given the form of horses.” said the ancient Greek philosopher Xenophanes, who grew wary of the depiction of the gods in Homeric epics. In fact many other philosophers as well were very ambivalent to the anthropomorphic (depiction of the gods as look like human beings) representations of the Greek gods in the classical literature. But some of them also revered the very literary works that were deeply interwoven in the fabric of society of ancient Greek. One the one hand they are genuinely convinced that the gods shouldn't look like humans, as they are represented in Homer and Hesiod works. On the other hand, these literary works are highly regarded and revered. So what should they do?

If the readers’ own understanding of the truth did not conform to the literal statements in the Iliad and the Odyssey, then they must discover a meaning to assign them. Even if the original composers, such as Homer, goal when composing those epics was not of rational and moral concern, the philosophers felt obliged to discover meaning in those works that aligns well with their conceptualizations of the gods. And that is how the world discovered what is now called allegorical interpretation. The presupposition of allegorical interpretation is that the word “signifies something other” than what is said, for it transmits a concealed meaning that possesses a deeper sense. Thus the interpreter's job is in unveil that  deeper sense that is obscured by the surface level meaning of the words. 

Looking at the origin of allegorical interpretation will be helpful in understanding the first century interpretations of Jews and Christians.

## Philo the Philo-sopher (get it?)
Arguably one of the most important Jew in Christianity, after Jesus, is the first century CE philosopher and diaspora Jew Philo of Alexandria. As a Jew himself he was well versed in Jewish study of the Holy Scriptures. But as a diaspora Jew living in one of the most Hellenized  cities of the ancient Mediterranean  world, Alexandria, he was also well trained in Greek philosophy. He read works of Plato, Aristotle and the Stoics.

 Working under Platonic philosophy — which says that the physical world we see is just a shadow, a temporary copy of a higher, eternal realm of spiritual realities (the World of Ideas) — Philo thought that truth is to be discovered only in a sphere that transcends what may be observed. Therefore, "If the writings of Moses [the Pentateuch] contain the truth, they must be interpreted according to that which resides behind the wording of the text in order for the actual meaning of the text to come forth." And to bring this transcendental meaning to light, one must employ allegorical interpretation. 

We should, however,  not that unlike the aforementioned ancient Greek philosopher who total rejected the literal meaning of Homeric literature in favor of the allegorical one, Philo actually thought a two-fold interpretation. One should observe the Sabbath and other commandments. But he also thought that behind those commandments and stories lies in a deeper meaning that transcends the observable reality. Because of this, Philo considered Moses to be the fountain of philosophy.  

One example of Philo's allegorical approach can be seen in his interpretation of the serpent in the Garden of Eden.The snake is the incorporation of ruinous sensual pleasure. In essence, Philo asserts: “Sensual pleasure is compared with the snake, for lust is as twisted and variously shaped as the movement of the snake.”

## Jesus Christ 
It should be obvious for everybody with a passing knowledge of Christianity that at the time of Jesus the Holy Scriptures were the Jewish scriptures, and not the New Testament.  And asking how Jesus interpreted the scriptures he considered sacred introduces as to one of the most contested issues in biblical studies. And that is the problem of sources. All the sources we have about the life and ministry of Jesus come decades after the events the sources purport to tell. As we have already established in the earlier installments of this series, the canonical gospels were written around and after the late 60s and early 70s CE. And Jesus' ministry ended in his tragic death around the late 20s. So math dictates there is at least 30 to 40 years of gap between Jesus' last breath and the evangelists' first ink. What this means is that there is decades of years through which stories and traditions of Jesus' life and ministry could be modified and embellished to correspond them to the needs of the post-Easter community. Because of this and many other problem, scholarship has always been in debate regarding to what extent the stories that we find in the gospels could be traced back to Jesus in the 20s CE. But the problem is that the question of the historical Jesus' interpretation of the Jewish scriptures entails that we know what Jesus himself said. But then again, scholarship has long disputed how certain we can be in tracing back the saying we find the canonical gospels to the historical Jesus. 

With that out of the way, we should,  however,  note, and also base our discussion on, the fact that biblical scholarship is in large agreement "that the announcement of the approaching kingdom of God, which was directly related to Jesus’ appearance, formed the center of his preaching." [^6]  Jesus' message can be categorized under the broad second Temple Judaism's recurrent movement known as Apocalyptic Judaism, in the sovereign God will defeat the forces of evil and return the kingdom of Israel. According to Jesus' version of apocalyptic teaching,  this act of God is not something that is longed for future. Rather it is immanent, and in fact, so immanent that it has already had broken into reality. If you have ever paid attention in church instead of sleeping (dont worry, I myself did a little to many sleeping ), you would notice that the second request in the Lord’s Prayer is directed to the kingdom’s imminent coming. 

When we look at Jesus treatment of the Torah, we can see that even tough he presupposed the  authority of the Torah, he sought himself as one whose authority surpasses the authority of the Torah. We, for example, find him re-examining the laws pertaining to murder and adultery, and tightening and broadening them by saying that whoever rages anger against the brother or covets desire in the look at a woman, has committed murder and adultery respectively. He radicalizes the Torah by leveling the strictness up and commands that his followers love not just their lovers, but also their enemies. In the many conflicts he had with his contemporary Jews (Pharisees and Sadduces ), we see Jesus overriding many laws that he and his followers were accused of violating. In one instance Jesus and his disciples were found plucking heads of sheaves on the Sabbath, and the Pharisees confront him because harvesting is prohibited on Sabbath. In response to their confrontation, Jesus proceeds to justify his deeds by alluding to the story of King David eating a sacred food while he[David] was on the run from Saul. Jesus' justification boils down to that if David had the royal authority to violate standard regulation under extreme circumstances, so has the Messiah of God's kingdom to override laws under necessary circumstances.

## Apostle Paul
It has sometimes has been argued by social media sensationalizers and fringe scholars that Paul, instead of Jesus, is the founder of Christianity. While this hypothesis has no place in scholarly circle, these people have some element of truth in recognizing the enormous influence Paul had and has in Christianity. He may rightly be called the second founder of Christianity. 

Just like Jesus, the Holy Scriptures for Paul and other early Christians were the Jewish scriptures. Paul himself was the first Christian author, whose writing later become recognize as authoritative as the Jewish scriptures in later decades. But Paul himself had at his disposal the Jewish scriptures. And he makes an extensive use of those  scriptures in his letters, specially the Letter to Roman, which contains about two thirds of his citations of Jewish scriptures.  

The fundamental principle that guided Paul's interpretation of the Jewish scriptures is the notion that each word of the Bible was to be understood as directed to the present. In consequence, Paul took many passages from them, , independent of their original context, and handled them as tough they were building blocks of his interpretation. "For example, in Rom 9:25–26 where he combines Hos 2:25bc and 2:1b to explain that the expression 'not my people' refers to the calling of the Gentiles to become the people of God."[^7] Paul is clearly taking that passage out of context. Because Hosea was originally addressing the people of Israel and their restoration, not the gentiles as Paul is using here. 


In Romans 10:6–8, Paul cites a passage from Deut 30:12–14. The Deuteronomy passage is concerned with the idea that the laws of God are not far removed from the ability of Israel, and that one doesn't need to be superhuman to observe those laws. But Paul comes and takes the original passage out of its immediate context, deletes verse 11 entirely removing the word "commandment", strips out the phrase "that we may hear it and do it" that was at the end of verse  12 to 14 and goes on and adds the phrase "Do not say in your heart..." from elsewhere as a preface to his statement. "This abbreviated and altered citation serves Paul’s process of thought, when he offers his commentary step by step: justice, which comes from faith, makes it unnecessary to repeat Christ’s ascent and his descent to the dead; his salvific act has occurred once and for all."[^8]


## Reflection 
As we have seen from the brief history of interpretation of the Jewish scriptures by different Jewish people in different time and place, there is not one single methodology by which people engaged in biblical interpretation. The Qumran community required their interpretation to strictly support their eschatological theology, regardless of the original composer's intent. Philo of Alexandria who was highly influenced by Greek philosophy sought to interpret the Torah through through Platonic metaphysics. Jesus Christ went so far as reexamining the Law to tighten it. Paul on other hand took parts of the Torah and appropriated them for his immediate theological needs, with no regard for the contexts in which they were being said, similar to how the Qumran community did. Modern day theologians are usually heard saying that theology should be only be done in such way that the texts are never taken out of context text. While I am myself a big advocate of reading the Bible in its own historical, literary and rhetorical context, this notion is not something that has been held consistently throughout the history of interpretation of the Bible, both in Jewish and Christian circles.
